#### Project: Brand Patterns

Tech Stack: 
	- front-end: TypeScript, Next.js, React, Redux, D3, TailwindCSS
	- analysis service: R, Plumber, Docker
Narrative: 
Internatinal advertising agency UM (Universal McCann) approached myself and a former colleague to build a streamlined tool to enabled their teams create custom visualizations for clients based on their proprietary Brand Patterns model. We were selected because during our time at Two-N we delivered a similar tool for sister agency Initiative and had experience working with their shared technical team and internal infastructure. Our task was to create a web application which could be used by agency teams to select their client, competitors, and audiences, translate those selections into a query to the YouGov API, pass the response into a service we would create to run their proprietery Brand Patterns model, and turn it's outputs into client-ready visualizations which could be viewed online or exported as static images as outputs. To achieve this we worked closely with their Chief Product Officer, the Analysts who created their Brand Patterns model, as well as their DevOps team. I took on a full stack role, working to turn the analysis manually-run scripts into a web service, managing the queries and database operations, as well as create a network visualization. My colleauge focused on the front-end, developing the majority of the UI as well as the Radar and Bar visualizations. For the analysis service we were asked to build based on scripts originally written in R. To accomplish this I used the plumber library to handle web requests and route them to the appropriate functions for data processing and retrieval and then used Docker to wrap this in a consistent environment which could be easily integrated with the client's infastructure. For the front-end, we chose a Next.js app to make use of it's API functionality for handling requests to YouGov and the analysis service. Redux was chosen to maintain user selection state across multiple pages and views, React was used for straighforward UI rendering and updates, with Tailwind CSS for efficient styling. Project data and settings were stored in a MySQL database connected to with Sequelize. Finally D3 was used to layout and render the radar, bar, and network visualizations. 
Client: UM (Universal McCann), part of Interpublic Group (Recently Acquired by Omnicom)
Industry: Advertising 
Link: https://www.umww.com/, (holding company: https://www.omc.com/)
Design Challenges:
- Streamlining brand and audience selection for complex queries, highlighting selections that could limit statistical validity.
- Designing complex comparisons for readability when exported as static images, while providing additional details and options as an interactive web app.
Tech Challenges:
- Arranging the layout of a complex network visualization to minimize link and label overlaps.
- Building a service to encapsulate analysts' manually run scripts into an internal service to process data and return a structured analysis output to the front-end for visualization.
Delivered Result:
A next.js web application that allows client teams to create radar and bar charts of comparative brand sentiment based on yougov data for customizable audiences and run advanced analysis based on an internal conversion propensity model visualized as a network diagram.

#### Project: Decoding Hate
Tech Stack: JavaScript, React, Three.js, react-three-fiber, Tween.js
Narrative: 
In the midst of the Covid-19 pandemic the team at Pitch Interactive was selected by The Asian American Foundation to help visualize the unprecedented amount of hate incidents targeting the AAPI community. Based on sketches from our Executive Creative Diretor, myself and another developer created a scrolling narrative and interactive timeline visualizing hate incidents as reported by news organizations and social media users visualized as a torrent of 3d fragments. My colleague primarily focused on the timeline element, while I was mainly responsibly for the scrolling narrative portion. Interface elements and text were created with React, while the fragments were rendered with Three.js, integrating them with react-three-fiber and using Tween.js to create refined and smooth animations. The flood of fragments animate in at the start of the experience to convey the magnitude of how many hate incidents are happening to the AAPI community. By utilizing color, sort features and interactive capabilities viewers can explore every fragment categorized by the types of hate incidents described in the tweet or news report chronologically from the start of the pandemic to present.

#### Project: The Artists Way
Tech Stack: JavaScript, React, React-Spring, Three.js, React-Three-Fiber, Tone.js, VOSviewer
Narrative: 
Collaboration with Artist Andrew Demirjian, I was primarily responsibly for the 3d visuals and Andrew for the generative audio. 
What it is: The language space, nodes and links
At the core of our explorations is the language space. We created this by taking our collection of artists' statements and
analyzing them using software called VOSviewer. VOSviewer is designed for the analysis of scientific publications,
highlighting co-occurrence and connections in key concepts extracted from a body of literature. Applying that to our
artist's statements gave us a network of nodes, meaning the spheres labeled with text, and metrics to position them
according to concepts like conceptual similarity of number of occurrences, as well as links, the curved lines between
them, showing when concepts appeared together. The Diptych often displays this visualization quite directly.
What it is: Exploring 3D virtual camera movement and poetry
Over time we experimented with a variety of ways to show this network, and to use its contents to a poetic end. Very early
on we were struck by the way that this collection of concepts and the connections between them resembled a landscape.
And given that, we pursued several options for moving the camera to both highlight a collection of words or a single word,
as well as to show the broader sweep of the conceptual landscape. This also presented technical challenges that we
needed to overcome, for example, keeping all the desired text in view, as well as timing and easing our camera motions to
avoid movements that were too abrupt or dizzying for the viewer.
What it is: Collective mind of digital media artists
To zoom out a bit, what we’ve tried to share is a glimpse into a sort of digital media artists’ collective subconscious. In
showing these parallels and recurring themes, we’re seeking both to lay out those patterns, and to critique and prod in a
bit of a playful manner, to encourage us all to reflect on why it is that so many of us return to the same kinds of language
when we talk about our work.Process: What tools created the diptych?
In terms of tools for the diptych, as well as for many of our early prototypes, we created them mainly using JavaScript and
other web technologies. The primary ones being React, which we used for state management, keeping track of things like
what sort of phrases we’re selecting or which display styles we want to use, as well as Three.js, which is responsible for
rendering the 3d scene. I’ll also say that we found a tool called react-three-fiber that makes getting these 2 tools to work
well together much easier.
Process: How did you arrive at the designs?
Our design decisions for the piece were driven by some key constraints and priorities. We knew fairly early on that a
network of some kind would be a natural way to show the relationships between concepts. We also knew that text would
be a major focus so legibility was an important consideration. Of course we also experimented with some distinct looks,
like pushing the nodes to look more like glowing stars or clouds, but those ultimately didn’t serve our goals as well.
Process: What are the challenges of working in 3D in the browser?
So we do have these great tools that make it possible to work with 3D in the browser, but there are still challenges
associated with it. Probably the most significant is performance. The exact constraints will vary depending on the
machine that’s running the piece but in general but biggest challenge was in keeping motion smooth, without hitches or
stutters when a large number of elements are on screen. In the diptych for example when the camera pulls out there are
hundreds of circles and lines onscreen. The primary strategy we used to address this was to limit what elements update.
The links, for example, are created once when the page loads and no aspect of them changes, they don’t move, they don’t
change color, and so on.
From Andrew's Website:
The Artist’s Way is an audiovisual navigation through the language space of artist statements created by Andrew Demirjian and James Proctor. The installation remixes over 400 artist statements of self-identified practitioners of digital/electronic arts practitioners to produce a generative three-dimensional poem, creating a linguistic hopscotch across the collective mind of digital artists. The Artist's Way consists of two large-scale projected artworks that draw upon the history of poetic structures to generate rules for parsing and sequencing the 16,000 words to form a new visual poem.  By re-positioning the meta-language of artists' statements as the art itself, The Artist’s Way aims to create a space to contemplate the role that systems of language play in our engagement with art. 
Both the diptych and the vertical wall use a rotating series of rules to parse and remix the artist statement language, each segment lasts roughly one to two minutes. Some of the longer phrases come from rearranging all of the sentences alphabetically by the first word of each sentence. We also excerpt and display all of the aphorisms and questions from the artist statements. 
Other parsing rules include searching the data base for references to a frequently used word or combination of words along with the next word, like ‘I + ____’, ‘my work + ____’, ‘this + ____’. Sometimes parts of speech analysis was used to make combinations of words or phonetic similarities were used to pair words. We also drew upon poetic forms like acrostics and haikus to create poems from the language. 
The Artist’s Way is part of a lineage of artworks that are institutional critiques of the art world. This piece is a critique of the role of language in our contemporary art ecosystem from gallery wall text to press releases to funding organizations to graduate MFA program instruction to artist statements. 
The idea for The Artist’s Way came from jurying a high-profile grant for digital media artists and having to read over 400 statements in a short period of time. I was struck by how similar so much of the language was and thought it was a clear example of how power works to shape a field – even one where originality is privileged – to conform to a rigid code. Often times, this body of language seemed so far removed from the sensations of awe, wonder and curiosity that art can produce.

Narrative: 
Retrofit presents an altered view of the built environment around SOMArts. A shifting composite of satellite and street-level imagery produces an uncanny reflection of the first person experience of the place, blended with the omnipotent perspective, familiar from digital maps. 
The piece is installed on the wall, consisting of a high resolution monitor mounted at eye level. The display reveals a scene generated from satellite tiles and depth-mapped images recombined in randomized positions, then stretched and bent out of proportion. Over time these composite buildings and streets slide and curve past one another, recombining into unreal, but familiar, streetscapes.
Retrofit is a game about living in an uncertain world. The player presses buttons to raise the elevation of the onscreen topography. Over time the landscape responds by reshaping itself; changing, and then erasing, the players work.
Presented as an arcade game, complete with cabinet and custom control scheme, Retrofit’s playful form invites viewers to manipulate its environment. As they work, sandy islands grow together and fill with trees, streets, houses, and hills. But their control is temporary and eventually these forms shift and erode away.
This piece is called Retrofit, and I showed this last year at a group show at SOMArts. It’s presented as this arcade game, and on the screen there’s a rough, polygonal landscape. You have this 8 by 4 grid of buttons, and each one corresponds to a point in the landscape. When you press one of the buttons, it increases the elevation of that point, and as the elevation increases, the landscape changes. So it starts off with just ocean and waves, and as you build upward you get sand, and trees, and houses and mountains.
But the catch, and where the name comes in is, you’re not the only one playing. The landscape is changing itself too. And you might notice this before you start but, little islands will build up and then sink back down. And you’ll definitely notice it eventually, because as you build up more it’s biased to sink those formations back down over time.
And so the interactions are relatively simple, you can change this environment, but then it goes right on changing itself in response - you can’t win or lose, you can just make stuff and then watch it fade away. But, because it feeds into this larger system, you don’t really know what you’re doing in the fullest sense, and your control is temporary. The changes you make last for a while, and then they’re undone.
